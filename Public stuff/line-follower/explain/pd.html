Line Follower · how the controllers work · PD

PD (proportional-derivative)

Steer in proportion to the error, and against how fast it is changing.

The signal every controller works from

The light sensor points down and sits over the edge of the line, so its reading depends on how far the car has drifted toward the line or away from it. On the Calibrate tab you measure the reading fully on the line (\(r_{\text{on}}\)) and fully off it (\(r_{\text{off}}\)). The target \(r^*\) is halfway between them, and the error rescales the distance from the target so that ±1 means fully on or fully off:

$$r^* = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(r_{\text{on}} + r_{\text{off}}) \qquad e = \dfrac{r^* - r}{\tfrac{1}{2}\,(r_{\text{off}} - r_{\text{on}})}$$

So \(e = +1\) means the sensor is well onto the line, \(e = -1\) well off it, and \(e = 0\) means it is exactly on the edge, where we want it. The controller's whole job is to steer so that \(e\) stays at zero. It does that by speeding one wheel up and slowing the other down by the same amount \(u\) (the steering) around the base speed \(B\):

$$v_L = B + u \qquad v_R = B - u$$

Following the right edge, a positive \(u\) turns the car away from the line; following the left edge the sign flips. The controllers differ only in how they decide \(u\) from the error.

The idea

Bang-bang has only two outputs, so it can't ease off as it nears the target. PD makes the steering a smooth function of the error: a big error gets a big correction, a small error gets a small one, and zero error gets none. It has two parts.

  • P (proportional): steer in proportion to how far off you are right now.
  • D (derivative): steer against how fast the error is changing. If you are rushing toward the target, the D term pushes back before you overshoot.

The rule

$$u = K_p\, e + K_d\, \dfrac{de}{dt}$$

\(K_p\) is how many percent of steering you get per unit of error. With, say, \(K_p = 20\), being fully on the line (\(e = 1\)) steers by 20, being halfway there steers by 10, and sitting on the target steers by 0.

The derivative is estimated from the last two readings:

$$\dfrac{de}{dt} \approx \dfrac{e_{\text{now}} - e_{\text{before}}}{\Delta t}$$

It is the speed at which the error is changing, in error-units per second. A positive value means the error is growing (the car is moving further onto the line), so \(K_d\) adds a bit more steering away from it; a negative value means the car is already coming back, so \(K_d\) takes some steering away to stop it overshooting.

Why the D term matters: a spring and a shock absorber

Picture the car as a mass on a spring. \(K_p\) is the spring: it pulls the car back toward the edge. A spring alone makes the car swing back and forth through the target forever. \(K_d\) is the shock absorber: it resists the speed, so the swings die out. Too little damping and it still wobbles; too much and it is sluggish and noisy.

The knobs and a way to tune them

The starting values on the PD tab are not arbitrary: they are computed from the steering authority \(b\) (Calibrate tab) so that the car responds at the target loop speed with a well-damped, quick response: \(K_p = 100\,\omega^2 / b\) and \(K_d = 100 \cdot 2\zeta\omega / b\) with \(\zeta \approx 0.7\).

SettingToo smallToo big
\(K_p\)Loses the line on curves; slow to correctWobbles back and forth, then oscillates wildly
\(K_d\)Still wobbles after a correctionAmplifies sensor noise: the wheels twitch

A good recipe. Set \(K_d = 0\). Raise \(K_p\) until the car follows the line but starts to wobble. Then raise \(K_d\) a little at a time until the wobble settles. Back \(K_p\) off if it gets twitchy.

What you will see on the plot

  • The motor lines now take many different values, rising and falling smoothly with the error, instead of flipping between two.
  • When the car is on the target the two motors run at nearly the same speed and it drives almost straight.
  • On a long steady curve a P-only controller settles with a small constant error: it needs some error to produce the steering the curve demands. (Adding an integral term, the “I” in PID, removes that offset. We haven't added one here.)

Where it is in the code

The PD tab's code card: compute the error, estimate how fast it is changing, add the two terms, and apply the result to the wheels. The time between readings is never allowed below 0.01 s, so a pair of readings that arrive almost together can't produce a huge fake derivative.
